# A five-minute English pronunciation practice routine

Use a phone or browser recorder and a short sentence. This is a suggested practice routine, not a promise of improvement in five minutes. No scoring service is required.

If you use Pronunciation Coach for an assessment, choose a sentence supplied by the app. The independent examples below are not custom text that can be pasted into its checker.

## Minute 1: choose one thing to listen for

Start with a sentence you understand. Identify one focus: the end of a word, a vowel contrast, a sound you find difficult, or the word carrying the main message.

For example, in “Please send the file today,” you might focus on the end of “send.” Do not attempt to fix vowels, speed, stress, and every consonant at the same time.

Where a reference recording is available, listen once for meaning and again for your chosen focus. Do not treat every difference from one speaker as an error.

## Minute 2: rehearse without scoring

Say the sentence in comfortable chunks, then join it back together. Keep the intended meaning in mind. If it becomes harder to say when you speed up, return to a comfortable pace.

Rehearsal is not a test: you can pause, restart, or listen again.

## Minute 3: record and replay

Find a reasonably quiet place. Make a short recording and listen before uploading it anywhere.

Check:

- Can you hear the whole sentence, including the first and last words?
- Is the microphone rubbing against clothing or a desk?
- Is the recording distorted, very quiet, or interrupted?
- Did you read the intended sentence?

Fix the recording first if needed. A score on unusable audio will not tell you much about your pronunciation.

## Minute 4: make one change

Listen for your chosen focus. If you have tool feedback, use the specific word or sound indication as a clue, then compare it with what you hear.

Write a concrete action: “Keep the ending of ‘send’ audible” is more useful than “Speak better.” Record another attempt under similar conditions.

## Minute 5: compare and stop

Compare the two recordings. Can you hear the change you intended? Did the sentence remain natural and understandable?

If a score changed but the audio does not sound meaningfully different, do not keep resubmitting just to chase a number. Save one observation and one focus for next time in the [practice log](../PRACTICE-LOG.md).

## A small independent rehearsal set

These sentences are original practice prompts for your own recorder. The focus is optional, and none is a diagnostic test.

| Prompt | Possible focus | Listen for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Please send the file today. | Word endings | Whether the end of “send” is lost |
| I need a seat near the window. | A vowel you find difficult | Whether “seat” sounds as intended |
| I think this is the right place. | The two English TH sounds | How “think” and “this” differ |
| We can meet after lunch. | Comfortable phrasing | Whether you break the sentence in an awkward place |
| I asked for the blue one. | Meaningful stress | Which word carries the correction |
| First, let me explain the problem. | Presentation opening | Whether the first phrase is clear and unhurried |
| Could you repeat the last number? | A clarification request | Whether the request is easy to follow |

For “I asked for the blue one,” try stressing **blue** to contrast it with red, then **I** to contrast yourself with someone else. There is no single correct stress pattern without context.

## When feedback is inconclusive

Keep the recording conditions similar and try again later, or ask a teacher or conversation partner what they heard. A listener who does not know the sentence in advance can help identify where the message becomes unclear. Do not share a recording without considering your privacy and theirs.
